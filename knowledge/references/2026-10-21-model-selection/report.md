# Which model, and which unit of work, makes reading and auditing Bulgarian legal text both cheaper and more reliable?

Source log: `/references/2026-10-21-model-selection/sources.md`. The run feeds the architecture decision of 21 October 2026 (decisions D-065 to D-068 in the repository's decision log) on how the reasoning run reads materials of Държавен вестник, the Bulgarian State Gazette, and audits the 3,624 acts of the corpus. Terms used throughout: the *reasoning run* is the model-driven step that decides what a text means; the *adjudication read* is the same step done in the owner's Claude Code session on the cases the cheaper steps could not settle; a *leg* is one kind of work (attribution, labelling, consolidation); the *catch-up* is the reading of the Gazette issues published since the corpus was last updated; the *backfill* is the reading of every Gazette material since 2003; the *daily loop* is the scheduled run that keeps the corpus current; a *tag-stripped source* is a web page with its markup removed and its text kept.

## 1. The question and the answer

The owner asked for a model choice between Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 for three legs of work (reading a Gazette material, consolidating an amended provision, auditing an existing act against its source), and rejected a sampled measurement in favour of what the model documentation and the published practice say. The owner also asked how legal-text applications and the Legalize country repositories handle the same work.

The answer is that the model choice is the wrong first question. The sources that bear on each point agree. On length: performance falls as the input grows, for every model tested, even on simple tasks, so the unit of work must be small (section 3). On output: in the one study that measured a model applying gazette amendments to statutes, 50.3 percent of single-step and 20.51 percent of multi-step results matched the official text exactly, so the model must return pointers and edits, never the text (section 3, extract#10). On trust: the United Kingdom's state consolidator leaves textual substitutions to editors, the European Free Trade Association's model pilot carries "no legal value", and no Legalize country self-consolidates from a raw amendment stream (section 4, extract#28). Once the unit is a provision and the output is a pointer, a string equality (the cited blocks must re-assemble the whole source) catches every omission and duplication without reading, and the cheapest model that passes that check can carry the bulk. That design is in section 5, and its cost in section 6: the audit of the whole corpus comes to about 45 dollars at list price, against about 860 for whole-act reading on Opus 5.5 (Reasoning, item 7).

## 2. What the model documentation says

Anthropic's own guidance for long inputs is to put the document first and the question last, and to have the model quote before it acts: "Place your long documents and inputs near the top of your prompt, above your query, instructions, and examples. This improves performance across all models" (extract#1), with "Queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests" (extract#2), and "ask Claude to quote relevant parts of the documents first before carrying out its task. This helps Claude focus on the relevant content and ignore the rest of the document" (extract#3).

The quoting advice has a mechanical form in the API, the citations feature, with one limit that matters here. "Citations are guaranteed to contain valid pointers to the provided documents" (extract#21), and the cited text "does not count toward your output tokens" (extract#22). But the pointer's granularity is the document's: "For plain text documents: Content is chunked into sentences that can be cited from" (extract#42), whereas "For custom content documents: Your provided content blocks are used as-is and no further chunking is done" (extract#43) and their citations "include the content block index range (0-indexed) corresponding to the original content list provided" (extract#44). So a pipeline that wants the model to point at its own splitter's blocks must send them as custom content blocks, and the pointer it gets back is a block index range, not a character offset.

The three current models are positioned differently. Claude Haiku 5.5 "is built for high-volume, latency-sensitive work such as classification, routing, extraction, and subagent tasks" with "a 1M token context window, and up to 128k output tokens" (extract#18). Claude Opus 5.5 "is built for long-running agentic coding and knowledge work" (extract#19). Claude Sonnet 5.5's effort levels were recalibrated against its predecessor, so a setting cannot be carried over (extract#20). None of the pages claims a measured long-context accuracy for its model, so the positioning is the vendor's intent, not evidence of reliability on this task.

Prices, from the pricing page: Opus 5.5 at 4 dollars per million input tokens and 20 per million output; Sonnet 5.5 at 2 and 10; Haiku 5.5 at 0.10 and 0.50 for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, and 0.50 and 2.50 above that threshold (extract#15); Fable 5.1 at 10 and 50 (extract#40). For Haiku, "A request's prompt length counts all of its input tokens, including cache reads and cache writes" (extract#17). The Batch API gives "a 50% discount on both input and output tokens" (extract#16), "with most batches finishing in less than 1 hour" (extract#23), limited to "100,000 Message requests or 256 MB in size" per batch, and "Batches expire if processing does not complete within 24 hours" (extract#24). Two consequences: a Haiku request must stay under 100,000 tokens to keep its price, which a per-provision unit does by a wide margin; and an audit that is not urgent should run as a batch at half price.

## 3. What the research says

**Long inputs degrade every model tested.** Chroma's report evaluates "18 LLMs, including the state-of-the-art GPT-4.1, Claude 4, Gemini 2.5, and Qwen3 models" (extract#33), holds the task fixed and varies only the input length: "models do not use their context uniformly; instead, their performance grows increasingly unreliable as input length grows" (extract#4), and "Across all experiments, model performance consistently degrades with increasing input length" (extract#5). The cause is the length itself: "By holding the needle-question pair fixed and varying only the amount of irrelevant content, we isolate input size as the primary factor in performance decline" (extract#6). Topically related material hurts further: "Even a single distractor reduces performance relative to the baseline (needle only), and adding four distractors compounds this degradation further" (extract#7). On conversational inputs the models "are highly capable of succeeding on the focused inputs" and then degrade "with the full inputs" (extract#9). One observation concerns two Claude models of that generation: "Claude models consistently exhibit the lowest hallucination rates. Specifically, Claude Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 are particularly conservative and tend to abstain when uncertain" (extract#8); the study predates the 5.5 models, and the run does not extend it to them. The run's reading of what this means for a statute, whose provisions resemble each other, is Reasoning item 1.

**A model applying amendments matched the official text exactly in half the single-step cases.** The study the search found on a gazette amendment stream used "a dataset of 908 amendment–law pairs drawn from 140 Federal Law Gazette documents across four major codes" (extract#34) and GPT-4.1-mini (extract#11). "While automated metrics show high textual similarity (93-99%) for single-step and multi-step amendment chains, only 50.3% of exact matches (single-step) and 20.51% (multi-step) could be achieved"; the authors "argue that any public or private deployment must treat outputs as drafts subject to rigorous human verification" (extract#10). By code the exact-match rate ranged "between 2.36% and 75.93%", and of 100 imperfect outputs "51% of errors had minimal to moderate impact, with 78% requiring only trivial corrections" (extract#11), so most non-matches are small; the authors nonetheless hold that "even small divergences can carry legal significance" (extract#10). Chains fail by propagation: "If Amendment 1 is not used correctly, Amendment 2 cannot, e.g., locate the text "10,000 euros" because this phrase only exists in the amended version, not the original" (extract#13). The earlier French work used a "light quantized generative model, finetuned with LoRA, to generate accurate and reliable amended texts" (extract#35) and reached "a success rate of more than 63% on a difficult bill" (extract#14); the German authors note of it that "context window restrictions prevented processing over half of the real amendments" and that it "handled only single amendments, not the sequential chains typical in real legislation" (extract#12). Both studies had the model generate the amended text. The run's reading of why that fails is Reasoning item 2.

**Structure-aligned chunks work best on statutes.** The one study of chunking a civil code for retrieval found that "chunking strategies aligned with the inherent legal structure - particularly section and subsection - based retrieval-achieve the highest recall, while more complex approaches that override this structure perform worse" (extract#26). A structured-prompting study of long legal documents likewise splits "each document" into "chunks via a system of chunking and augmentation, addressing the long document problem" (extract#32). Both are retrieval settings, not consolidation, so they show where the natural unit lies, not the accuracy of applying an amendment inside it.

## 4. What practice does

**The United Kingdom's state consolidator keeps text edits with editors.** Its editorial system "can apply certain effects automatically, such as repeals, commencements and non-textual amendments" but "textual substitutions are applied by editors" (extract#31). The deterministic replay compiler studied in June, run over "690 fully-replayable amended statutes" against the official Finnish consolidation, reached a "mean Levenshtein text distance 0.65%" and a "mean section-level structural error 4.25%" with "104 below 90%" agreement (extract#30, extract#36), and in that design "the residual gap is the finding, attributed to a lane, not silently absorbed" (extract#37).

**The first official pilot with a model is explicitly non-authoritative.** The EFTA Secretariat "is conducting a pilot project to explore how AI‑based tools can support the consolidation of EEA legal acts"; it "began on 15 January 2026 and will run for a period of six months", and its "consolidated versions are provided for information purposes only and have no legal value" (extract#25).

**No Legalize country self-consolidates.** The June research on the ecosystem found that "No Legalize country self-consolidates from a raw amendment stream. None." (extract#28): "All examined countries (Spain/BOE, France/LEGI-DILA, EU/EUR-Lex, Germany/gesetze-im-internet, Sweden/Riksdagen-SFS, South Korea/National Law Information Center) fetch already-consolidated text and official version history from a state-run source" (extract#38), and the specification's only answer to a missing official consolidation is "periodic re-download to accumulate history post-hoc" (extract#29). Spain's repository is "every law is a Markdown file, every reform a commit. 8,600+ laws" (extract#27), whose "Data is obtained from the BOE's (Boletín Oficial del Estado) open data API" (extract#39). Bulgaria is the only member that has to consolidate, so there is no country pipeline to copy for the reading step; the ecosystem's practice is the fetch-and-transform plumbing this repository already has.

## 5. The design this supports

The unit of work is one provision with its source span, never an act. A deterministic splitter cuts the tag-stripped source into candidate blocks by the markup lex.bg and the Gazette already carry (the semantic CSS classes on lex.bg, the paragraph tags on the Gazette). The splitter decides nothing about meaning: it only proposes boundaries, and every character of the source lands in exactly one block. The blocks are sent as custom content blocks, so that the citations the model returns are block index ranges into the splitter's own list (extract#43, extract#44).

The model's job per block is to label and to point: this block is an article heading, a paragraph of article N, an item, a section heading, an annex row, or a quoted passage inside another provision; and here are the block indices it covers. A script then assembles the Markdown from the labelled blocks. The round-trip check is mechanical and total: the concatenation of the cited blocks, in order, must equal the source text. An omitted block, a duplicated one, or an index the model invented fails that equality without anyone reading. What the check cannot judge, and what the model decides, is the label; that decision is recorded with its citation so a later reader can re-check it.

For an amendment, the model reads the amending instruction (a few hundred tokens) together with the target provision it names (a few thousand), and returns an operation, not text: the address, the kind (replace, insert, repeal, renumber), the exact old string cited from the provision and the new string cited from the instruction. A script applies it. The check is again mechanical: the old string occurs exactly once at the address, and after the edit the new string is present and the old is not. The instruction forms that do not reduce to a cited string pair are returned as "cannot apply" with the reason, and they go to the adjudication read. This is the division the United Kingdom's editors use (extract#31), with the model where the editor sits and the script where the system sits; the retyping that both consolidation studies relied on (extract#11, extract#35) does not occur, which is the run's reason to expect the exact-match failure not to occur either (Reasoning, item 2).

With the unit that small and the check that strong, the model choice becomes a question of which model passes the check often enough, measured by the check itself on every block, not by a sample. The bulk labelling runs on Haiku 5.5, whose documented purpose is "classification, routing, extraction" (extract#18) and whose list price is a fortieth of Opus 5.5 under 100,000 tokens per request (extract#15). A block the round-trip rejects, or that the model labelled with low confidence, is sent to Opus 5.5 for a second labelling; a block the two disagree on goes to the adjudication read in the session. Amendment operations run on Sonnet 5.5 first, with the same escalation. Every escalation is counted, so the error rate of each model on each leg is a number the pipeline reports after every run, which is the measurement the owner declined to buy separately, obtained for free. The error rate that matters, a wrong corpus text, is bounded by the mechanical checks regardless of the model, because no text reaches the corpus that the checks did not reassemble from the source. A Gazette material is read the same way: its attribution (which act it touches) reads its title and opening blocks, and its instructions are read one block at a time, so no request exceeds the Haiku threshold whatever the material's length.

## Reasoning

1. **Read per provision, never per act.** Rests on extract#4 to extract#7, extract#9 and extract#33 (degradation with length and with distractors, isolated as the cause, across 18 models) and on extract#26 (structure-aligned units give the best recall on a civil code). Inference: a statute places the Chroma design's two adverse conditions together, many near-identical passages that act as distractors for one another and a question with low similarity to any one passage, so reading a whole act is the less reliable option whatever the model.
2. **Return pointers and edits, never text.** Rests on extract#10, extract#11 and extract#13 (half exact match for single steps with 93 to 99 percent similarity; chain propagation), extract#14, extract#35 and extract#12 (the generative predecessor at 63 percent, limited by context), and extract#21, extract#22, extract#43 and extract#44 (citations are valid pointers, cost no output tokens, and point at custom blocks by index). Inference: high similarity with low exact match is what a model produces when it re-emits text it was not asked to change; a design in which a script assembles text from cited blocks and applies cited string edits has no re-emission to fail. The inference is the run's, not a finding of either study.
3. **The completeness check is mechanical and total.** Inference from the design of item 2: if every output is a block index of the source and the blocks must re-assemble the source exactly, omission and duplication are decidable by string equality, which is a check on the fixed part of a built file in the sense of the owner's rule of 21 October 2026, not a pattern over prose. Only the label stays a judgement, and it is recorded with its citation.
4. **Model choice is decided by the check, per block, not by a sample.** Rests on extract#15 and extract#18 (Haiku's price and documented purpose) and on item 3. Inference: with a total check on every block, the cheapest model may carry the bulk and its failures are counted, so the error rate of each model on this task is reported by the pipeline rather than estimated. Extract#8, on abstention, is about Claude Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 and is not applied to Haiku 5.5.
5. **The session's own measurements**, not from a source: the corpus size, measured on 21 October 2026 by concatenating every act file (236,534,615 characters); the raw lex.bg page of the Constitution at 722,023 bytes against its 146,470-byte corpus file; the 151 Gazette materials of бр. 82 to 96 of 2026 at 2,393,560 characters of text in all, two of them over 300,000 characters, from the run manifest `data/runs/catchup-2026-10-21/manifest.json` of this session; the title triage of those 151 (55 to read, 14 to read with a scope question, 82 not read) in `data/runs/catchup-2026-10-21/triage.json`, with about 35 of the 69 expected to amend a corpus act. Tokens are estimated at three characters per token for Bulgarian.
6. **Practice supports the division of labour.** Rests on extract#31 (the United Kingdom's editors apply substitutions, its system applies repeals and commencements), extract#30, extract#36 and extract#37 (a deterministic compiler over 690 statutes still leaves 104 below 90 percent agreement, each a finding), extract#25 (the first official model pilot carries no legal value), extract#28, extract#29 and extract#38 (no Legalize country self-consolidates; all fetch state-consolidated text). Inference: none of these systems lets a model's consolidated text stand without a check; the design in item 2 and item 3 is the checkable form of what the practitioners do by hand.
7. **Tens of dollars on Haiku, hundreds on Opus, never thousands.** Derived from the figures of item 5 and the prices of extract#15: whole-act reading of source plus current Markdown, about 176 million input tokens on Opus 5.5 at 4 dollars with about 8 million output tokens at 20, is about 864 dollars; a whole-act re-render, about 96 million input and about 90 million output on Opus 5.5, is about 2,184 dollars; the per-block design of section 6 is about 45 dollars with its escalation on Haiku 5.5, about 292 on Sonnet 5.5 and about 584 on Opus 5.5 for every block. The saving against whole-act reading comes from the output, not the model: the design writes labels, about 10 million tokens, where a re-render writes about 90 million.
